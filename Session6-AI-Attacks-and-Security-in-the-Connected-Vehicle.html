Orientation

Three faces of AI security in a vehicle

Before diving in, hold this map in your head. "AI security" in a car isn't one thing — it's three relationships between AI and an attacker. Every topic in this session lives in one of these boxes.

FACE 01

Attacks ON the AI

The car's own models — perception, path planning — are the target. Fool the camera, spoof the LiDAR, poison the training data.

FACE 02

AI AS the weapon

The attacker holds the AI. LLMs reverse-engineer firmware, generate exploits, and automate CAN-bus attacks at machine speed.

Module 01

Fooling the eyes: attacks on in-vehicle AI

QUICK TAKE

The car's cameras and sensors feed a neural network that guesses what it sees. Change the input just enough — stickers, laser pulses, poisoned data — and the guess goes wrong. You never touch the code; you attack what it perceives.

A self-driving stack doesn't "see" a road — it runs camera, LiDAR, and radar data through neural networks that output guesses: that's a pedestrian, that's a stop sign, that lane is clear. Attackers target the guesses.

Scenario

A few weatherproof stickers, placed just so on a stop sign, cost a couple of dollars. To a human driver the sign still says STOP. To the car's classifier, it now reads "Speed Limit 80." The car doesn't slow down.

First, how does a car "see"?

Perception fuses three complementary sensors. Each is strong where the others are weak — and each can be attacked in its own way.

Fusion is a strength and a weakness — corrupt one input and the whole decision can tip.
adversarial example

An input that has been changed by a tiny, carefully chosen amount — often invisible or meaningless to a human — that causes an AI model to confidently produce the wrong output. The math finds the exact pixels that push the model over a decision boundary.

The classic result: add imperceptible noise to a photo of a panda and an image classifier calls it a gibbon with 99% confidence. In a car, the "panda" is a stop sign and the stakes are physical.

⚑
Note the failure path. Push perturbation past ~85% and the sim flips to "attack too visible." That's real: a sticker big enough to break the model is also big enough for a human safety driver — or a second sensor — to notice. Good attacks are subtle; good defenses raise the cost of subtlety.

Beyond stickers: the wider attack menu

  • Sensor spoofing. Skip the model entirely and lie to the sensor. Shine crafted laser pulses at a LiDAR to inject fake obstacles, or blind a camera. GPS spoofing feeds a false position to navigation.
  • Data poisoning. Corrupt the training data so the model learns a flaw. If fleet data flows back to retrain models, a poisoned stream can teach the whole fleet a bad habit.
  • Backdoor / trojan. A hidden trigger baked into the model — a specific pattern that, when seen, forces a chosen wrong output while behaving normally otherwise.
  • Model extraction. Repeatedly query a model to clone it, then hunt for its weaknesses offline where nobody is watching.
Real-world use case

Researchers have physically fooled production-adjacent driver-assist systems: projected phantom images made a car "see" a pedestrian that wasn't there, and lane-line stickers nudged vehicles toward the wrong lane. None required touching the car's software — they attacked what the sensors perceive. This is why defense-in-depth pairs redundant sensors with sanity checks (does LiDAR agree the camera's "sign" is even a flat object at sign height?).

"You can't patch a model into never being fooled — you engineer the system so being fooled isn't enough."

Module 02

Turn the tables: AI in the attacker's hands

QUICK TAKE

AI doesn't invent new attacks — it deletes the labor that made them rare. LLMs read firmware, fuzzers find crashes, and forged CAN frames slip onto a bus that trusts everyone. Cheaper attacks mean more attacks.

Flip the relationship. Now the attacker holds the AI. The same models that make cars smart make attackers faster — compressing weeks of manual reverse-engineering into an afternoon.

Scenario

An attacker dumps an unfamiliar ECU's firmware, pastes the disassembly into an LLM, and asks: "What does this function do, and where does it trust input it shouldn't?" Minutes later they have a plain-English map and three candidate weak spots.

Where AI accelerates the attacker

  • Reverse-engineering firmware. LLMs summarize disassembly, rename mystery variables, and explain unknown protocols — lowering the skill floor to attack an ECU.
  • Automated fuzzing & exploit synthesis. ML guides fuzzers toward inputs likely to crash a component, then helps shape a crash into working control.
  • CAN-bus attacks at machine speed. Learn the "normal" rhythm of a bus, then inject forged frames timed to win arbitrationOn CAN, if two ECUs transmit at once, the message with the lower ID wins the bus. Attackers time forged frames to win this race. and override a real ECU.
  • Social engineering & deepfakes. Cloned voices and tailored phishing target the humans around the vehicle — dealership techs, fleet operators, OTA signing staff.
CAN bus (Controller Area Network)

The decades-old broadcast network most ECUs talk on. Every node hears every message, and classic CAN has no authentication — a frame claiming to be from the brakes is trusted as if it were. This is the soft underbelly attackers aim for once they're inside.

Why the flood is different. The injection lies; the flood just shouts — jamming the bus so real safety frames can't get through. Both matter. The defense against both is the same idea: give frames an identity classic CAN never had (see AUTOSAR SecOC in Module 4).
Real-world use case

The landmark 2015 remote-vehicle demonstration chained an infotainment weakness to the CAN bus and controlled steering and brakes from miles away — triggering a 1.4-million-vehicle recall. Nothing about it required AI. But every step it took by hand, today's tools can help automate: mapping the firmware, finding the pivot, crafting the CAN frames. AI doesn't invent new physics of attack — it removes the labor that used to make attacks rare.

The attacker's advantage was never genius. It was time. AI is time.

Module 03

AI on your side: catching the frame that doesn't belong

QUICK TAKE

A car's network is wonderfully predictable, so anything off-rhythm stands out. ML intrusion detection learns "normal" and flags the rest. The catch: too twitchy = false alarms, too relaxed = missed attacks. Tuning that dial is the job.

A car's networks are beautifully predictable — the brake ECU speaks every 10 milliseconds, no more, no less. That predictability is a gift to defenders: anything that breaks the rhythm is suspicious. AI is very good at learning rhythm.

Scenario

An injected "brake release" frame arrives 3 milliseconds early and carries a value the brake ECU has never sent in a million miles of driving. A machine-learning intrusion-detection model flags it in under a millisecond and quarantines it — before the actuator ever moves.

IDS — Intrusion Detection System

Software that watches network traffic for signs of attack. An ML-based automotive IDS learns the normal statistical fingerprint of the CAN bus — message timing, frequency, value ranges, who-talks-to-whom — and raises an alert when live traffic drifts from that baseline. It's anomaly detection: you don't need to have seen the attack before, only to notice "this isn't normal."

The defender's dilemma: two ways to be wrong

An IDS lives on a knife-edge. Set it too twitchy and it screams at every harmless quirk — false positives — until the fleet operator mutes it. Set it too relaxed and a real attack slips through — false negatives. Tuning that threshold is the job.

The teaching moment. Nudge sensitivity to 100% — you'll catch every attack but drown in false alarms; to 0% — dead silent, and every attack lands. Security engineering is rarely "make it perfect." It's "choose the failure you can live with, and layer other defenses behind it."

Where the alerts go: the VSOC

One car's IDS is a smoke detector. A whole fleet reporting into a Vehicle Security Operations Center (VSOC) is a fire department. The VSOC correlates alerts across thousands of vehicles, spots a coordinated campaign, and pushes a defensive over-the-air update — turning one car's bad night into the whole fleet's immunity.

One vehicle detects · the fleet becomes immune.
Real-world use case

Major automakers now run VSOCs modeled on enterprise security operations centers, ingesting telemetry from connected fleets. When an anomaly pattern appears across many vehicles, analysts can distinguish a buggy sensor from a genuine campaign and respond with an OTA fix in hours instead of a physical recall in months. UN R156 makes this OTA-update capability a regulated expectation, not a nice-to-have.

Module 04

The rulebook, lightly: who says you have to care

QUICK TAKE

Don't memorize standards — know which one answers which question. Some say you must manage the risk, some name the AI-specific risks, some are how you build the defense. One map, below.

You don't need to memorize standards to pass this session — you need to know which one answers which question. Here's the map. Each framework exists because one of our three faces became a real, regulated risk.

GOVERNANCE

ISO/SAE 21434

The backbone standard for automotive cybersecurity engineering — manage cyber-risk across a vehicle's whole lifecycle, from design to decommission.

REGULATION

UN R155 / R156

Law, not advice. R155 mandates a cybersecurity management system; R156 mandates safe software (OTAOver-the-air: pushing software to vehicles wirelessly, like a phone update — no dealership visit needed.) update management. Required for type approval in many markets.

MESSAGE TRUST

AUTOSAR SecOC

Gives CAN frames the identity they never had — authentication + freshness so a forged "brake" frame is rejected. The direct answer to Module 2's injection.

ADVERSARIAL AI

MITRE ATLAS

A knowledge base of real tactics against ML systems — evasion, poisoning, model theft. The "attacker playbook" for Face 01.

AI RISK

NIST AI RMF

A framework for managing AI risk — trustworthiness, robustness, monitoring. Vendor-neutral and pairs with the adversarial-ML taxonomy.

AI + SAFETY

ISO/PAS 8800

The emerging bridge between AI and vehicle safety — how to argue an ML-based driving function is safe enough to ship. (Complemented by ISO/IEC 23894 for AI risk generally.)

◎
One-line mental model. 21434 & R155/R156 say you must manage the risk. ATLAS and NIST AI RMF tell you what the AI-specific risks are. SecOC and ISO/PAS 8800 are how you actually build the defenses.

Module 05

Defense-in-depth: no single wall, many nets

QUICK TAKE

No defense has to be perfect, because they stack. The same forged-brake attack dies four times over — auth, IDS, sensor cross-check, fleet learning. Layered defense turns "possible" into "not worth it."

Every attack we met has a counter — and the point is that no counter has to be perfect, because they stack. Watch the same forged-brake attack from Module 2 die four different deaths.

Layered defense turns "possible" into "not worth it."
Security isn't a wall you finish building. It's the practice of never betting everything on one net.
